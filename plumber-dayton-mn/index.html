Who we serve

Dayton at a glance

Dayton had 7,262 residents in the 2020 census, and it lies mostly in Hennepin County with a small corner in Wright County. The Old Village dates to the 1850s, and newer subdivisions have grown around it. About 36 percent of homes were built since 2010, and 93 percent are owner-occupied, so most of what we see is newer plumbing with the problems that come from builder-grade fixtures and sump systems.

Water and septic

City water, private wells and septic systems

Dayton's municipal water serves the Historic Village, the southwest industrial park and the northeast area. Everything else is on private wells. The city's wellhead plant removes iron and manganese from the water it supplies, according to the city.

Roughly 885 septic systems operate in Dayton, and the city's ordinance calls for inspection every three years. We repair and replace plumbing up to the tank, and we say so when a problem belongs with a septic contractor.

Local note

What this means for repairs

A home on a well may have iron, manganese or hardness that the city treats for its own customers. Those minerals scale water heaters and clog aerators, so a water test comes before any treatment decision. See water softener and filtration for how to decide.

A home on septic has a different failure pattern than a home on sewer. Slow drains across the house can mean the tank or the drain field, and a camera or a pumping visit tells you which.

Common calls

What Dayton homeowners call us about

Sump pump failure

New subdivisions near the rivers and creeks can have high groundwater after storms. A failed pump needs fast attention. See sump pump repair.

Water heater trouble

Well water with minerals shortens tank life, and builder-grade tanks installed since 2010 are reaching the age where failures start. See water heater replacement.

Drain backups

Shared drain lines in newer homes can back up after years of grease or wipes. See drain cleaning.

Hidden leaks

A water bill that jumps, or a well pump that never rests, can mean a leak. See leak detection.

Before we arrive

Before We Arrive in Dayton

Find the shutoff

On a well system, the pressure tank shutoff or the main valve stops water. City-water homes have a main valve near the meter.

Turn off the pump if needed

For a leak on a well system, switch off the well pump breaker so the pump does not keep running.

Protect belongings

Move items off a wet floor and photograph the damage.

Keep access clear

Clear a path to the water heater, the sump pit or the cleanout.

If you smell gas, leave the house and call 911 or CenterPoint at 612-372-5050 before you call us.
